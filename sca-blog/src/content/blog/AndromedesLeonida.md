<h2 id="intro">Intro</h2>
Background if you're not familiar with the SCA. If you are, feel free to <a href="#references">skip ahead</a>.

For those of us in the Society for Creative Anachronism (SCA) we often celebration people's achievements within the various activities that we do. The highest levels of achievements are known as elevations into Peerage. This recognition takes many years to achieve and is awarded through polling of others in that activity and review of the participants efforts. 

When these elevations happen there is a number of items which have traditionally come to be awarded. For example, a white belt for the peerage of chivalry, or a set of archer's bracers (forearm protection) for the archers obtaining the Order of the Mark peerage. These regalia items are bestowed to the participant in our mock court, and often takes months of preparation. The elevation includes something called a vigil where the participant sits to receive vistors throughout the day. People's vigils often happen in a tent for privacy and those tents are elaborately decorated.

For Andromedus those individuals responsible for his vigil asked that I work to create a sculpture which he could have in his vigil tent and to enjoy for many years to come. In discussions, I was given a 3D scan of a sculpture which resides in the Louvre. That original sculpture is that of Antoninius Pius. When looking at this sculpture I noticed something unusual that the back of the sculpture was shaped akin to a bronze sculpture, including a rod in the center. This would be highly unusual for a second century bust which would be made of stone. [1] This is not to say a bronze bust could not have been made at that time, though there have been none found on a small scale of approximately two feet/60cm. 

<h3 id="references">Finding references and the process of making the piece</h3>

I was commissioned to create a sculpture for a friend's knighting vigil. Although the initial request was for a 3D print, I recommended plaster for a more authentic look and feel, especially since Andromedes, the recipient, is interested in the Roman Republic era. Receiving a bust is period-appropriate, since between 500 and 27 BCE Roman senators and military figures of note commemorated achievements with portrait sculptures. [1]

To begin, Sir Gilchrist, my sponsor for this project, sent me a 3D scan of a bust of Antoninus Pius from the Louvre. Upon closer inspection, I realized the scan may have been of a Renaissance-era piece, not an ancient original, but it still provided a useful reference. I found additional sculptures of Antoninus Pius and identified a Roman-era artifact that was likely the Renaissance sculptor’s inspiration. Continuing that tradition, I drew inspiration from the Pius bust in both antiquity and the Renaissance piece. With these references, along with Andromedes on my monitor, I set to work digitally.

I used the 3D scan as a base, cleaned up major artifacts, and modified the bust’s structure so it would work better as a 3D-printed plaster cast. I sculpted Andromedes’ head from scratch, blending it into the form, much like how ancient sculptures were sometimes repurposed for new commissions. [2] My digital approach mirrored this historical practice, where old, unfavored politician sculptures were reused by removing the head entirely from the bust or remaking the head. [1,2] In this case, no ill intent toward Antoninus, though Andromedes needs his bust, and time was of the essence.
Knowing the risks of plaster casting and mold overhangs, I first created a small maquette test. This trial run broke during demolding, but I found the result accurate based on found artifacts and kept the breaks unrepaired. The neck had broken, and I also lost the nose. It taught me where the weak points were. For the final sculpture, I addressed weak points by cutting and rejoining sections after casting, the head, torso, and base. Although my first plaster pour produced bubbles in the head, I fixed them later and reinforced problem areas with a thicker, putty-like plaster mix. I used both traditional and custom-made metal and wood sculpting tools to refine the details, then sanded the surface, filling new bubbles as they appeared, and learned the hard way not to rush the drying process with plaster of Paris.

With repairs complete, I assembled the three main sections using a metal rod for support, inspired by Renaissance techniques. [3] Drilling plaster requires patience to avoid breakage, another lesson learned from the maquette, so I worked slowly and by hand with no power tools. Connecting the head to the torso was arguably the worst challenge of the full build. Even with careful fitting and extra plaster, movement of the piece always caused cracks. I finally bridged gaps with plaster-soaked paper for added stability, then smoothed and reinforced the join. It bulked it up poorly, though I did my best to reduce the bulk. Future portraits in this style will incorporate this into the sculpting and finishing process.
After final wet sanding, I sealed the sculpture with several coats of acrylic paint to protect it from moisture and add some flexible durability. I believe the finished piece pays homage to Roman portraiture through modern and traditional sculpting techniques, so Andromedes has something to celebrate his achievement for years to come.
